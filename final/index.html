01   Abstract

Summary

This project combines rendering techniques with mesh generation to recreate the visual effects of snow accumulation within an arbitrary scene. To approximate the visual effects of snow, such as glints and subsurface effects, we used a triplanar texture mapping, which solved issues regarding the lack of UV mappings in our generated meshes. To model the buildup of snow in a scene, we used signed distance fields with a surface nets algorithm. Together, these effects can place a layer of glinty snow on any mesh scene.

02   Technical Approach

How We Built It

Microfacet BSDF Foundation — Charlie Herbert

Most of the project effort was initially focused on attempting to implement realistic snow glint effects, following the work of Kemppinen et al. To this end, we began by setting up a microfacet BSDF within the homework 3 framework. We used both the 2020 version of the instructions for project 3 and the work of Walter et al. as references to guide our implementation of the microfacet BSDF. These provided the necessary algorithms, as well as an outline of which functions we’d need to implement. From here, we simply implemented the normal distribution, self-shadowing, and fresnel functions. This part went fairly smoothly, though it took slightly longer than expected due to some coordination issues.

Glint Model Attempt — Kemppinen et al. — Charlie Herbert, Allena Ogilvie

The next step was to implement the glint algorithm from Kemppinen et al, which takes the normal distribution function of the microfacet renderer and converts it to a ‘glinty’ distribution. However, we needed to overhaul the HW3 solution to support differential rays for this algorithm. To do this, we used the equations found in Homan Igehy’s Tracing Ray Differentials and created our own datatype that extended the existing ray structure. Then, the HW3 pathtracer was reworked to use the new reflect and translation functions. This seemed simple at first, though later debugging revealed several small programming errors in the initial implementation, which wasted a significant amount of time later on. In particular, a custom function for reflecting a differential ray into a provided direction was found to be calculating the normal between the input and output directions incorrectly.

Ultimately, the Kemppinen glint implementation didn’t work out. Due to a lack of group resources for debugging the glint algorithm, the end result suffered from significant visual errors. While we had chosen Kemppinen’s approach for its efficiency and seemingly straightforward implementation, the conversion from a shader program to our raytraced renderer proved too challenging given the constraints. We did find that applying just the importance sampling from the Beckmann distribution of the microfacet BSDF produced a decent ‘glinty’ look for some simple cases, though it resulted in noisy specular artifacts being scattered around the scene.

The lack of promising results from the Kemppinen glint BSDF prompted a late switch to a simple, texture-based snow BSDF. To do this, we used the texture data type from homework 1, copying over the ‘Texture’ class and functions for file loading. Sampling the textures was done using triplanar mapping, which had been implemented as part of the Kemppinen approach. For the triplanar equations, we adapted Martin Palko’s code for triplanar shaders in Unity to work with our framework. We did need to modify the equations to support tiling with our texture sampler, though this was easily accomplished by rounding and rescaling our world-space coordinates.

SDF Design - Jeffrey Fang, Sophia Liu

To implement snow accumulation upon our scene, we used signed distance fields (SDF). The inspiration for this idea came from the work of DreamCat Games, who explained how to create smooth meshes for cubic scenes through an algorithm called Surface Nets. Essentially, a voxel grid stores an SDF at each point, indicating the closest distance to a surface, our snow, where negative values are defined to be ‘inside’ the surface. Using these, we can linearly interpolate the positions of the surface points inside each cube of 8 voxels. A surface passes between two voxels when the sign on their SDFs flip. When we find such a ‘voxel edge,’ we create a quad with the 4 surface points around the edge as shown [above? on the right] with the red lines. This quad is then split into two triangles, and added to the mesh.

In addition to finding surface vertices, the SDF also allows us to compute normals with respect to the surface. The normal of a vertex on the isosurface is simply the gradient of the SDF at this point. While this makes computation simpler, we still had to implement bilinear interpolation to calculate the gradients of points inside our voxels. By computing the change in the SDF along all 12 voxel edges, we calculate the surface normal by using a weighted average of the gradients at the corners of our voxel. Doing this allows us to apply our snow BSDF to our resulting mesh.

To actually apply this method to a given scene, we first use raycasts to determine where snow actually accumulates. Based on the surface normal of the intersecting object, we determine how much snow to “deposit” in a sphere at that location. Deposition consists of creating an SDF of a snow blob at the hit location, then merging it with our existing SDF by taking the minimum of the two values. As a result, the isosurface for the final SDF traces the outer boundary of all the snow in our scene, meaning that applying the Surface Nets Algorithm we implemented gives a full triangle mesh for our snow.

One important parameter we varied in our testing was how the size of the snow decayed with increasing slope. If the size did not decay fast enough, we would get unrealistic overhangs of snow off of objects. However, if it decayed too fast, the snow spheres were not connecting to form a continuous mesh, becoming chunky instead of smooth. The correct granularity of our voxels with a linear decay gave us the realistic rendering of snow landing on objects.

Problems Encountered

Communication and pacing were the biggest coordination challenges. Two separate implementations of the snow accumulation algorithm were developed in parallel before the team converged, costing significant time. A more granular roadmap with individually delegated intermediate deadlines would have substantially helped. On the technical side, the incorrect normal computation in reflect_from_next_dir in the differential ray implementation caused difficult-to-trace visual artifacts that delayed the glint work by over a week. Earlier visual testing would have surfaced this faster -- a noisy 16-sample render identifies most shading bugs far more efficiently than code review alone.

Footprint SDF Carving — Allena Ogilvie

The proposed footprint carving was implemented using SDF subtraction. It is negating ellipsoid SDFs and merging with max instead of min to carve depressions into the snow surface. The geometry of the depression itself worked, having a boot and a foot shape that were distinguishable. Also, the carve depth was controllable through the Y radius of each ellipsoid component. Although sound in theory, the visual results had two main problems that made them unconvincing. The first is the coordinate system depth variation. A real footprint in the show is not a uniform flat depression. The heel sinks deepest, the arch barely touches, the ball of the foot creates a secondary ridge, and individual toes leave distinct, rounded pockets at varying depths. Our SDF carving used simple ellipsoidal unions, which produce a smooth bowl-shaped subtraction at each component of the render. There was no way to uncode the non-uniform vertical pressure profile of a real footstep without significantly more complex SDF composition. This would require a drastic amount of time to tune correctly and more communication of the development progress.

Secondly, the carving broke the snow material illusion. A render of the snow footprint is more akin to a footprint cutout in dough, not in snow. The current f() function samples a snow albedo texture via triplanar mapping, blending across three projection axes using power-of-4 weights. The depression creates a hard geometric boundary where the carved region meets the undisturbed surface. Without spatially varying the albedo sample or the specular contribution based on how compressed it is at each hit point. This would require reading each SDF value at dr_in→o during shading. As a result, the footprint reads as a geometric hole rather than a compression in the snow material. The material looks identical inside and outside the footprint, which breaks the illusion entirely. In real snow, a compression has a different crystal structure from the noncompressed surrounding area. The compressed snow is denser, icier, and reflects light differently from the fresh powdery snow. Given the time remaining and priority of getting a high-quality baseline snow accumulation render correctly, the footprint was cut from the final deliverable.
